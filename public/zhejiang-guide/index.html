开场与概况｜先交代在哪里、有什么特色

Ladies and gentlemen, welcome to Zhejiang. I am happy to be your guide today. Zhejiang is on China's southeastern coast, in the southern part of the Yangtze River Delta. Hangzhou is its capital. The province takes its name from an old name for the Qiantang River.

Look at the variety of landscapes: green hills, rivers, lakes, towns and islands. Zhejiang has a long coastline, while much of its inland landscape is hilly. This variety gives visitors many different ways to explore the province. We can enjoy a lake, walk through an old town, visit an ancient site or travel to the coast.

To remember Zhejiang, keep three words in mind: water, culture and trade. Water shaped the landscape and daily life. Culture tells us what people created. Trade connects the province with the wider world. As we follow the scenes, we will see how these ideas belong together.

水与生活｜自然不是背景，要说明它怎样影响人

Let's begin with water. Rivers and lakes supported farming and helped people travel. Before modern roads and railways, boats carried goods between communities. Water was therefore both a natural resource and a practical route.

In a water town, notice the relationship between houses, bridges and canals. A bridge allows people to cross, while boats travel along the water. Narrow lanes provide another route beside the homes. Together, these features explain how a town worked. The view is beautiful, but it also grew out of ordinary needs: carrying food, going to work and visiting neighbors.

Beyond the towns, the hills and forests offer a different experience. They remind us that Zhejiang is not only a province of lakes and canals. Its natural variety is an important part of its tourism. When we look at these scenes, we can ask one simple question: how has the landscape shaped the way people live?

历史文化｜从良渚到今天的手艺与日常

Now let's turn to culture. Near Hangzhou, the Archaeological Ruins of Liangzhu City take us back more than five thousand years. Rice farming, large earth structures, water-management works and fine jade objects help us understand an early society. These are archaeological clues, rather than a complete ancient city still standing above ground.

Jade is a stone valued for its beauty and cultural meaning. The carefully made objects from Liangzhu tell us about skill, belief and social status. The site became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2019. It is an important place for understanding the early development of Chinese civilization.

Culture also continues in everyday life. Tea, silk, local food, opera and traditional crafts give visitors different ways to experience Zhejiang. Notice the work of the hands, the colors of a costume or the shape of a ceramic object. These details make culture easier to understand than a long list of unfamiliar names. They also remind us that traditions depend on people learning and passing on skills.

代表资源｜把西湖与大运河讲具体

Two important places bring our ideas together. The first is West Lake in Hangzhou, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2011. Its special quality comes from the relationship between nature and human creativity. Hills and water form the setting; causeways, gardens, poems and stories enrich it.

A good example is the Su Causeway. It is linked with Su Dongpo, a poet who also served as a local official. Mud removed from the lake was used to build the causeway. A project that improved the lake also created a beautiful place to walk. That is an easy way to understand a cultural landscape: practical work and natural beauty become part of the same view.

Our second example is the Grand Canal. It is a network developed over many centuries, rather than one channel dug at one time. Boats carried grain and other goods, while towns grew along the banks. At Gongchen Bridge in Hangzhou, people cross above the water and boats pass through the arches below. The bridge helps us picture the connection between transport and daily life.

West Lake invites us to enjoy a landscape. The canal helps us understand how places and people were connected. Both are beautiful, but they tell different stories. This is why choosing two contrasting examples is useful when we first get to know Zhejiang.

今日浙江与结束｜贸易只作一段，文化旅游仍是主体

Today, modern transport and business continue the story of connection. Yiwu is a well-known example of trade in everyday goods. Products may be small, but they link designers, makers, sellers and customers in many places. Its wholesale market can be understood as a place where shops do their shopping.

For a visitor, Zhejiang can also be understood through simple experiences: watching a craftsperson at work, trying a local dish, listening to opera or sharing a cup of tea. We do not need to understand everything at once. One well-chosen experience can make the province feel personal.

So remember our three words. Water shaped life. Culture gives it meaning. Trade connects it with the world. I hope you enjoy both the famous sights and the small details along the way. Please take care near water and follow the visiting rules at heritage sites. That concludes my commentary. Thank you.

开场与概况｜解释大运河，落脚浙江段

Ladies and gentlemen, welcome to the Grand Canal in Zhejiang. Look at the boats moving along the water. A scene like this helps us begin with a practical question: before trains and trucks, how could people move large amounts of grain over long distances? Water transport offered an important answer.

The Grand Canal is a network of waterways developed and connected over many centuries. It was not all built by one ruler at one time. It carried goods, supported cities and brought people into contact. The canal became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2014.

Hangzhou is an important part of the story, but the Zhejiang section extends beyond Hangzhou. The Eastern Zhejiang Canal links inland waterways towards Shaoxing and Ningbo. Today, we will use three places to understand this heritage: Gongchen Bridge, the Grand Canal Museum and the Qiaoxi historic neighborhood. Together, they show transport, history and daily life.

水运背景｜有船才有桥、有货才有街区

First, notice that a waterway is more than water between two banks. Boats need places to stop. Cargo needs to be loaded, stored and sold. People working along the canal also need food, homes and services. Over time, these activities help shape the surrounding city.

Grain was especially important because towns and cities needed reliable food supplies. Other goods also moved along the routes. With the traders and boat crews came news, skills and local customs. The canal therefore connected both economies and cultures.

As different bridges and buildings appear, think about their different jobs. A crossing helps people move from one bank to the other. A landing place connects a boat with the shore. A storehouse keeps goods until they are needed. We do not have to remember every building name to understand how the system worked. But we should look closely at the main landmarks, starting with Gongchen Bridge.

主要点一：拱宸桥｜形制、功能与历史

This is Gongchen Bridge, one of the best-known landmarks on the canal in Hangzhou. It is a stone bridge with three arches. Its history goes back to the Ming dynasty, with the original bridge built in 1631. The bridge has also been repaired and rebuilt over time, so its long history should not be confused with every stone being unchanged.

Notice the high curved openings and the path over them. Boats move along the water below while pedestrians cross above. The bridge joins the two banks without simply blocking the water route. Its shape helps us understand its purpose.

From the bank, we can see the whole curve. From the bridge, people gain a wider view of the canal. These are two different ways to appreciate the same structure. Imagine a boat approaching the arch and a person carrying goods across the steps. Both depended on this place in their daily work.

The bridge is therefore more than a convenient subject for a photograph. It shows how a city had to accommodate movement in two directions. Please watch your step and leave space for others when stopping on the bridge.

主要点二：博物馆｜由眼前一段水读懂整个网络

Our next stop is the Beijing-Hangzhou Grand Canal Museum. A museum visit adds something that a riverside view alone cannot provide: a clearer picture of the canal's development and its place in people's lives.

When we look at a map or a historical display, we can connect a small local stretch with the larger network. The water in front of us may look ordinary, but it belongs to a much wider story of engineering, transport and exchange. That change of scale is the main reason to include the museum in our visit.

A useful way to explore is to ask three questions. Where did the goods come from? How were they moved? Who depended on their arrival? These questions turn names and dates into a human story. They also help us understand why maintaining a water route mattered to people far away from the place where repairs were made.

主要点三：桥西街区与结尾｜从运输回到生活

Now let's turn to Qiaoxi, the historic neighborhood west of Gongchen Bridge. Its name is easy to remember: qiao means bridge and xi means west. The canal and the neighborhood developed a close relationship through work and daily life.

Notice the streets, doorways and buildings beside the water. A historic district is not only a collection of attractive fronts. Its spaces tell us how people moved, worked and met one another. The small scale of a lane gives a different experience from the broad waterway beside it.

The final scenes also broaden the story towards the waterways and cultural traditions of eastern Zhejiang. Images of boats and people pulling them remind us of the labor behind transport. Literary images remind us that waterways also became routes of travel and inspiration. We should not mistake every later scene for another street beside Gongchen Bridge.

Let's bring our visit together. Gongchen Bridge shows how people crossed the canal. The museum helps us understand the wider network. Qiaoxi shows the life that grew along the banks. Please keep a safe distance from the water, and follow the crew's instructions if travelling by boat. That concludes my commentary. Thank you.

开场与概况｜先解释“文化景观”

Ladies and gentlemen, welcome to West Lake in Hangzhou. Before reaching for your camera, take a moment to look at the relationship between the hills, the water and the city. This is a place where a natural setting and human creativity have developed together.

West Lake became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2011. It is recognized as a cultural landscape. In simple words, its value includes both nature and the things people have added: causeways, gardens, bridges, poetry and stories. A causeway is a raised path across water or low ground.

Today, we will focus on three places: the Su Causeway, the Bai Causeway and Broken Bridge. They are easy to connect in our memory because each offers a way to experience the lake. One helps us understand a great public project, another offers a famous lakeside walk, and the third brings scenery and legend together.

湖景过渡｜从整体看向具体的堤桥

Notice how the view changes with the weather and the season. Clear light brings out the hills. Mist makes distant forms softer. Trees, reflections and moving boats add different layers. The lake is not experienced in exactly the same way on every visit.

Traditional scenic names often suggest a season, a time of day or a particular mood. They encourage us to notice something specific instead of simply collecting photographs. Spring flowers, summer lotus leaves and winter snow all offer different experiences.

The causeways also change the way we see the water. Rather than staying at one viewpoint, we can move into the landscape. A short walk changes the relationship between nearby trees and distant hills. Keep that idea in mind as we begin with the Su Causeway.

主要点一：苏堤｜一个人、一项工程、一种景色

The Su Causeway is named after Su Shi, also known as Su Dongpo. He was a famous poet of the Song dynasty and also served as a local official in Hangzhou. His connection with the lake was practical as well as literary.

When he led work to clear mud from the lake, the removed material was used to build the causeway. This helped improve the lake while creating a route across it. Six bridges connect sections of the causeway. Its history shows that a useful public project can also become a much-loved part of the scenery.

Look at the relationship between the path, the bridges and the trees. The bridges allow water to pass through, while the path lets people enjoy changing views. In spring, willow trees and blossoms add color and softness to the setting. This seasonal beauty is remembered in the traditional scene called Spring Dawn at Su Causeway.

We can remember this place through three ideas: Su Dongpo, care for the lake, and a beautiful walking route. He left Hangzhou more than poems. His public work also helped shape the landscape that people enjoy today. That is why the causeway belongs in both the city's history and its scenery.

主要点二：白堤｜与苏堤对照，避免人物与工程混淆

Our next example is the Bai Causeway. Notice the open water beside the long path and the trees along it. The combination of willows, seasonal flowers and broad views makes it a memorable place for a walk.

The causeway is associated with Bai Juyi, a poet and local official from the Tang dynasty. There is an important distinction to remember: today's Bai Causeway should not simply be described as the actual embankment built by Bai Juyi. The association honors his lasting connection with Hangzhou and West Lake.

Why introduce this causeway after the Su Causeway? They show two related but different links between a landscape and a famous person. At the Su Causeway, we can explain the lake-clearing project. At the Bai Causeway, we can talk about memory, poetry and the pleasure of walking beside the water.

For visitors, the experience can be very simple. Look across the lake, notice the trees nearby, and then compare the view after walking a little farther. You do not have to know a poem by heart to enjoy the landscape that inspired generations of writers.

主要点三：断桥｜名字、雪景与传说

Now let's turn to Broken Bridge, at the eastern end of the Bai Causeway. First, the name does not mean that visitors must jump across a gap. One popular explanation links it with the appearance of melting snow, when part of the bridge can seem to disappear from a distance. This is a traditional explanation, rather than a certain account of how the name began.

The bridge is also closely associated with the Legend of the White Snake. In the story, a snake spirit takes human form and falls in love with Xu Xian. Broken Bridge is connected with their meeting and reunion. This is a legend, not a historical event, but its themes of love and separation have made the place meaningful to many people.

The performance images remind us that the story also lives in opera and other arts. We therefore have three layers: a real bridge, a seasonal view and a story shared across generations.

Let's remember our three stops. The Su Causeway combines public work with scenery. The Bai Causeway connects a lakeside walk with cultural memory. Broken Bridge brings a view and a legend together. Please take care near the water and keep paths clear when taking photographs. That concludes my commentary. Thank you.

开场与概况｜先说明为什么这里看起来不是一座完整古城

Ladies and gentlemen, welcome to the Archaeological Ruins of Liangzhu City, near Hangzhou. Here, we explore an early city through the evidence left in the ground. We will not see a complete ancient palace still standing. Earth structures, waterworks, burial sites and objects help us piece the story together.

The city dates from around 3300 to 2300 BCE, more than five thousand years ago at its earliest stage. It became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2019. Liangzhu provides important evidence of an early regional state in the lower Yangtze River area, supported by rice farming.

Today, we will follow three connected subjects: water, the city and jade. Our main examples are the water-management system, the Mojiaoshan area, and the burials and jade objects associated with Fanshan. Each helps answer a different question: how did people support life, organize a city and express shared beliefs?

主要点一：水利系统｜先讲城市如何运转

Let's begin with the water-management system. The maps and reconstructed scenes help show the relationship between the city, surrounding hills and waterways. These images are modern explanations based on research; they are not views recorded in ancient times.

Large dams and other water-management works formed part of the wider landscape. Their importance comes not only from their size but also from what they reveal about planning. People had to understand the land, gather materials and organize labor. Such projects required cooperation over an area larger than an individual household.
